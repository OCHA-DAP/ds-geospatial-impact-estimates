Did SAR see the Langtang Lirung collapse coming?

On 26 August 2026 an ice–rock mass detached from the north flank of Langtang Lirung and ran ~100 km down the Lende Khola and Trishuli, killing hundreds. Two days later, this scan of six years of Sentinel-1 backscatter asks whether the failing face looked different beforehand. It did — starting four months out, and only in the morning.

1 · The scar is where the reports said it would be

The first post-event Sentinel-1 pass (orbit 85, 28 Aug, 36 h after collapse) scored against that orbit's own June–August 2026 stack lights up a detachment arc and a runout track heading northwest into the upper Lende valley — 9.4 km² of strong change in this 10 km scene. The change centroid inside the source box lands 260 m from the coordinate Dave Petley published on the Landslide Blog. Everything below analyses that face knowing, from the data itself, that it is the right one.

2 · The face left its six-year envelope in April

Mean VV backscatter over the source box, plotted by day-of-year for each of the three orbital tracks that image it, shows 2026 dropping out of the 2020–25 band from mid-April on the two descending (early-morning) tracks: melt-out came three to four weeks earlier than in any prior year, and the face then sat 1–2 dB below every previous monsoon all season.

3 · It's the face, not the weather

A warm year depresses backscatter everywhere. So every acquisition is scored against prior years at the same point in the season, for the source box and for two control boxes on the same massif matched in elevation and slope (4,973 m/40° vs 4,990 m/39.5° and 4,908 m/37°). The controls dip mildly (−0.8 to −1.0σ on average); the source face runs at −2.3σ, reaching −5.6σ.

4 · Morning passes see it; evening passes don't

Subtracting the controls from the source and scoring that difference against its own prior-year distribution removes valley-wide weather entirely. What remains splits cleanly by time of day: the two 06:03-local descending tracks show a sustained face-specific departure beyond −2σ from 2 July — eight weeks before the collapse (intermittent from late June) — while the 18:06-local ascending track never leaves its envelope until the event itself.

In monsoon, an evening pass always sees a wet, melting face — 2026 looks normal at 18:06. A morning pass normally sees a surface that refroze overnight. A depression only at 06:03 means the face was staying wet through the night: an anomalously warm, melt-loaded hanging glacier, visible in free SAR data months before it failed.

5 · What amplitude did not see

Inside the source box, the share of anomalous pixels within the future scar tracks the share just outside it on every orbit and every pre-event date — the anomaly is face-wide, with no concentration in the 1 km² that ultimately failed, and no acceleration in the final pass 39 h before collapse. Backscatter flagged which face was in trouble, not which block.

{{FIG:preevent_fraction}}

Fig 5Share of pixels ≥2σ vs the 2023–25 monsoon baseline, inside the future scar (blue) vs the rest of the source box (grey). The common June→July decline is a seasonal artefact of the pooled baseline; the two series never separate.

The motion question was tested directly. InSAR phase is off the table here (GRD amplitude only in this archive, and a melting monsoon face decorrelates within any 12-day pair anyway), but speckle offset tracking works on amplitude: windowed phase cross-correlation (640 m windows, 1/10-pixel subpixel) between the last two pre-event pairs per morning orbit — orbit 19's pair ends 39 h before the collapse — with one-cycle-earlier reference pairs as the noise floor. Whole chips carry a 2–6 m common co-registration offset between GRD products (the reference pairs are worse than the final ones, confirming it is systematic, not motion); after removing the surround's median shift vector, the face's differential displacement in the final pairs is median 1.4 m / p90 4.1 m — at or below the reference pairs' own noise (2.0 / 4.2 m). And 220–221 of 221 face windows stayed correlated, so this is a measured null, not a failure to measure: no sliding above ~2 m per 12 days at this scale, right to the end. That supports melt-driven sudden detachment over weeks of slow creep — and strengthens the case for the thermal proxy over motion methods for this failure type.

7 · How often would this cry wolf?

The natural objection: we analysed this face knowing it failed. So the detector was replayed prospectively over 45 comparable faces — every GLIMS glacier in the surrounding 130×90 km with 0.5–15 km² area, mean elevation ≥ 4,800 m and mean slope ≥ 25° — for five monsoon seasons (2022–26). Per face and morning orbit, each acquisition is scored against the other years (leave-one-out, ±12 days of day-of-year); the same-date fleet-median z is subtracted (the "everyone else" control, replacing the hand-picked boxes); an alarm is three consecutive acquisitions ≤ −2σ.

Two structural results ride along. Scale matters: at whole-glacier scale the source glacier's own 10 km² polygon scores only −1.5 and is missed — the anomaly dilutes, so an operational version needs face-scale tiles, which multiplies tested units and makes 1.3 % a lower bound. And the one 2026 fleet alarm may not be false: an uncollapsed face at 28.57°N 85.49°E — Tibet side, same Lende/Kyirong drainage, ~32 km north — is running at −7.5σ this season, stronger than the collapse face itself. Same regional forcing, or a face worth watching right now. The two 2024 alarms (−2.1, −2.4, Shishapangma area) had no known collapse and count as false.

Caveats, honestly

  • One region, five seasons, one collapse. The replay makes the false-alarm rate concrete, but a single true positive proves feasibility, not skill. The 2024 alarms show it fires in warm anomalies without a collapse — any operational use is as a watch-list ranker, not a siren.
  • Geometry. A 40° Himalayan face means layover and foreshortening; orbits are never mixed and the controls share aspect and slope, but absolute dB levels are not comparable across tracks.
  • Trend vs event. A multi-year warming trend would also push z negative — but Fig 2 shows 2026 below all six prior years, not the bottom of a drifting fan.
