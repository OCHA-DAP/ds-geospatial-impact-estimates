Making sense of
satellite damage assessments

LESSONS FROM THE 2026 VENEZUELA EARTHQUAKE · UN OCHA CENTRE FOR HUMANITARIAN DATA, DATA SCIENCE TEAM

On the night of 24 June 2026, two earthquakes, forty seconds apart, struck the coast of Venezuela. Within days, at least eight organisations had turned satellite imagery into assessments of building damage.

Advances in AI and satellite imaging have made assessments like these far faster and cheaper to produce, and more organisations than ever are producing them. The abundance is new, and the humanitarian system is still learning how to make use of it operationally.

Scroll ↓
The response begins
In the immediate aftermath, timely information is critical. the EU's Copernicus Emergency Management Service (Copernicus EMS) was activated the same night, and in the following days, while the response was still underway, satellite damage assessments began to arrive.
Search-and-rescue teams on a collapsed building, Caracas. Credit TBD.
Part one

The assessments arrive

The reference
Copernicus EMS was activated the night of the earthquake. Its mapping is done by expert analysts marking damaged buildings one by one on very-high-resolution imagery, inside the dashed areas; it arrived area by area over the following days, with the final products on 6 July. This is the reference the other assessments will later be measured against.
Day 2 · the first model
The same day, the first modelled assessment arrived: a building-level classification based on high-resolution optical imagery from Microsoft AI for Good. It flags 8,332 buildings in the coastal strip area.
Day 4 · radar
IMPACT Initiatives delivers a SAR (radar) based analysis. Radar works through cloud and darkness, covers a far larger area, and measures change rather than appearance. It flags 81,446 buildings.
Day 5 · a third view
A team from Oregon State University, working with NASA, delivers another SAR (radar) based analysis, with different methods and a different area of interest: 57,066 buildings.
Day 7 · two more
UNEP delivers another SAR-based analysis, this one relating detected damage to debris tonnage (75,476 buildings). Later that week, another assessment arrives from a collaboration between WFP, LIST and CERN (163,225).
Additional assessments
Meanwhile, the UN assessment and analysis group becomes aware of a further analysis by an academic group, UH SAIL at the University of Houston: a deep learning model based on optical imagery classifying buildings across eight coastal AOIs. 74,705 flagged. Two further products arrived in the same window but are not evaluated here: HOT's fAIr, an AI-plus-crowd product that publishes no analysed extent, so its misses and false alarms cannot be counted fairly; and DISHA (UN Global Pulse), which is licence-restricted and covers less than half of the shared strip.
The information coordination issue
A stack of assessments of the same disaster: PDFs, shapefiles, dashboards. Different methods, different extents, and answers from 8,332 to 163,225 damaged buildings, a factor of twenty. Which do you act on?
Where they can be compared
The outlines show where each assessment actually looked: areas spanning three orders of magnitude, and no two alike. The dashed white line is where the Copernicus EMS analysts worked (manual mapping on very-high-resolution imagery, not modelling). The small red strip is the one place all of them overlap: 61 km² and 57,241 shared buildings, where the assessments can be compared fairly, building by building. Even here, on identical buildings, the six count anywhere from 5,362 to 25,882 damaged.
Part two

Making sense of these different assessments

Three questions
This evaluation aims to answer three questions an emergency or search-and-rescue coordinator would ask:
  1. How good are the assessments at flagging damaged buildings?
  2. Do they rank neighbourhoods well enough to guide triage?
  3. How should they be used together?
The baseline
Inside that shared strip, we measured every assessment against a reference dataset built from three sources, each catching what the others miss: manually mapped damaged buildings (Copernicus EMS), crowd-sourced volunteer review (MapSwipe, 400+ volunteers), and reports from field teams on the ground (ChatMap). The teal dots are the 2,064 buildings where Copernicus EMS recorded damage. For every assessment we then asked: of the buildings it flags, how many are really damaged? And of the damaged buildings, how many does it find?
Ranking neighbourhoods
Zoom out, from buildings to neighbourhood cells of about 0.7 km² each. The colour shows how much damage Copernicus EMS recorded in each cell.
Ranking neighbourhoods: the answer
At this scale, every one of the six assessments ranks the cells fairly well on its own. That matters: search-and-rescue teams told us that in the first 72 hours, narrowing down the worst-hit areas was exactly what they needed most. One caveat carries through: this holds inside an area already known to be badly hit. Answer 2 Yes, inside a known disaster zone. Ranking orders that zone; it does not find one, and it never names a building.
Using the assessments together
In real time, as products arrive during a crisis, there is no definitive answer on which one to use or how accurate it will be. So rather than choosing, we count: for every building, how many of the six assessments flag it.
A cross-product composite
This agreement analysis indicates that there is promise in a cross-product composite that could give responders more meaningful, actionable building-level analysis. As we add more product-agreement requirements to the output, our confidence in each building flag grows and noise is lowered. On the map: the buildings where four or more assessments agree (3,114 of them). It needs no training data and no waiting; it works as soon as the second assessment arrives, and it improves with every one that follows, using more of the available information instead of betting on one source.
Combining also ranks best
Back to the neighbourhood ranking from question two, with one more row: the cells ranked by agreement between the assessments out-rank every single one of them. So combining helps at both levels that matter: fewer false alarms building by building, and a better answer to where first.
Using them together
Combining is not only more accurate, it is the only way to see the whole picture. Each colour is where one assessment looked, and no two covered the same area. During this response, an ad hoc viewer our team stood up within days let responders see who had looked where. It filled part of the gap, not all of it, and nothing had planned for it. Answer 3 Do not choose one; count agreement between them. Agreement removed most false alarms at building level and out-ranked every single assessment at neighbourhood level, and it needs a shared picture of who has looked where to work.

Conclusion

1 · Identifying building-level damage

Compared to our mixed reference dataset, all individual products appear to over-flag damaged buildings, to such an extent that they would be challenging to use at this response level.

2 · Ranking neighbourhoods for triage

Search-and-rescue teams told us that in the first 72 hours their pressing need was to narrow down the worst-hit areas, and this is where the products are strongest. Within a subset area already known to be heavily damaged, they rank neighbourhoods for triage fairly well (three of the six reach a Spearman ρ of 0.7 or more against the Copernicus EMS ordering). Evaluated against the full geographic scope each product delivered, their ranking ability declines markedly (see the technical brief).

3 · Using the products together

Combining products by counting agreement improves performance and lets stakeholders set the threshold that defines the balance of coverage against confidence for their operational needs at different stages of a response. This simple method shows promise and substantial improvement at both the building level and the neighbourhood-ranking level. And because, in real time, there is still no definitive answer on when to use which product or how accurate it will be, compositing is a way to cut back that uncertainty and use more of the available information for better estimates.

What this response demonstrated

Advances in AI and satellite imaging have made damage assessments cheaper and faster to produce, so future disasters will likely bring more of them, from more organisations. This crisis and the emergency response demonstrated the need for two things:

  1. A shared, live picture of which analyses have been produced and where they looked.
  2. An interpretive layer to help key stakeholders make sense of and evaluate new maps and analyses as they become available.

Recommendations

For responders and response coordinators: do not act on a single flagged building from any one product; trust the neighbourhood ranking within an already-identified damage zone, where it is most useful in the first days; rather than choosing a product, count agreement between them, and treat buildings where every product agrees but the reference is silent as priorities for field verification rather than false alarms (in this event they were mostly real damage); and keep one shared, live picture of what has been analysed where.

For providers: publish the analysed extent and per-building acquisition metadata, and keep a channel open for feedback during the activation while reprocessing can still help.

For evaluators: keep testing this methodology across different crises, and build the system that makes it routine: a required specification for contributed products (analysed extent, metadata, a common building base) and a standing way for providers to contribute into a shared space, so that combining and evaluating starts the day the first product arrives rather than weeks later.

Limitations

This is one earthquake, in one dense coastal setting, and the shared comparison area is only 61 km², with most of the reference damage in a single area. The references have gaps of their own: the crowd review saw only places the products had flagged, and the field reports are positives-only with no defined survey area.

The full method, every sensitivity analysis and the per-product results are in the technical brief.
